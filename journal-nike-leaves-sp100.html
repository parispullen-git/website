Why Is Nike Leaving the S&P 100? The Real Reason, Explained

It isn't a delisting, and Nike isn't going anywhere. But a company doesn't lose its seat among the hundred biggest names in America without the market saying something about why.

Before U.S. markets open on September 21, 2026, Nike will lose its seat in the S&P 100. Read quickly, that sentence sounds like the kind of thing that precedes a eulogy. Read correctly, it's an index-membership change — a piece of financial bookkeeping, not a verdict on whether the company survives the decade.

The mechanics are almost boring, which is the point. S&P Dow Jones Indices announced the change on September 4 as part of its routine quarterly rebalance. Nike exits alongside Honeywell Aerospace, Simon Property Group, and Colgate-Palmolive. Dell Technologies, Palo Alto Networks, Arista Networks, and SanDisk move in. Four consumer-adjacent and industrial names out, four infrastructure-and-security names in — a rotation, not a purge.

This is a change to index membership. It is not an announcement that Nike's shares stop trading.

The S&P 100 exists to track roughly a hundred of the largest, most liquid companies in the country — a narrower cut of the broader S&P 500, rebalanced periodically so the list stays honest about who's actually big right now, not who used to be. That's the entire mechanism. It doesn't ask whether a company is well run, only how much of it the market currently believes exists.

By that measure, Nike's answer has gotten smaller. The stock has fallen more than three-quarters from its 2021 peak, and the math behind that decline is not abstract — by some estimates it has erased on the order of $230 billion in market value, with shares recently trading near levels the company hasn't seen in roughly twelve years. Losing a seat reserved for the hundred largest companies in America is simply what happens to the math when the number underneath it shrinks that far.

What it isn't is a delisting. Nike remains a full constituent of the S&P 500, the index most retirement accounts and index funds actually track, and its shares keep trading exactly as they did the day before. A fund built to hold the broad market has no reason to touch its Nike position because of this change alone. The only funds forced to act are the ones built specifically to track the narrower hundred — and even there, the selling is mechanical, timed to the effective date, and over almost as soon as it starts.

The more honest read is the symbolic one. Nike held its place in the S&P 100 for nearly eighteen years — long enough that an entire generation of investors never saw the index without it. All four names replacing it come from the same direction: chips, cloud infrastructure, cybersecurity. That's not a coincidence so much as a receipt. It's where the market has decided the next decade of American corporate weight is actually sitting, and it isn't sitting in a swoosh.

None of which makes this nothing. For a company mid-turnaround, getting quietly moved out of the room reserved for the hundred biggest names in the country is the kind of marker that shows up in board memos long before it shows up in a press release. It just isn't the marker most people will assume it is when they see the headline — and the difference between those two readings is usually where the actual information lives.
