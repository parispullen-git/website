Welcome to the Penthouse — Here's Where Everything Is

Seven rooms, two floors, and a reason I kept every one of them. If you've never actually clicked through, start here.

Most of you land on this site and end up in one room without ever meaning to — the Living Room, twenty-seven floors up, because that's where the front door actually opens now. Nobody's given you the rest of the floor plan. So here it is, once, start to finish: seven rooms, two levels, and a reason I kept every one of them.

First stop is the smallest one on purpose. The Kitchen — black marble, brass, and this week there's a box on the counter I didn't order and didn't unpack myself. It just shows up, already put away like it always was there. I don't cook every night. When I do, I cook well, and at this point I've stopped asking how the box knows that about me.

Move right and you're in the room the site actually starts you in: the Living Room. Two storeys of glass, and if the fireplace is lit it's because someone was sitting here not long before you arrived. There's a journal left face-down on the counter that I probably shouldn't have left open, and a jacket over the back of the reading chair that never quite made it back to the Closet. See the dot sitting on either one? Every room's got a few of those — tap one and a card slides up with the real specs on whatever you're looking at.

A safe nobody can see is a safe somebody goes looking for. What's inside isn't paper.

The vault isn't hidden behind anything either — brass wheel, black steel, set right into the wall where you'd have to be looking for it not to notice it. What's inside is UR Welcome, and it isn't public yet. There's a candle on the back counter too, unlit, no launch date I'm confirming for you today. Both of them are staying exactly where they are until I'm ready.

Take the down arrow from the Living Room and you end up somewhere I didn't used to let anyone see — the Music Lounge, one floor below. Vinyl floor to ceiling on one wall, a turntable that's basically never off, and a bar through the archway on the left if you keep walking that direction. The record player's got its own dot, one playlist queued on shuffle and left running — not the same rotation as the Remote's Music tab, which plays the same thing in every room. This one's just for in here.

Back up and keep going right and you're in the Cinema — nine seats, one screen, and a phone rule that's actually enforced, unlike most of what I've written down for myself upstairs. The posters on the wall are all one register: men in tailoring, making decisions, usually badly. I'll call it research. It's partly research. There's a Remote button in the corner too, and it's not decoration — tap it and you get three real tabs: TV, Music, Cinema. Hit Guide under TV and you'll find the actual channel list — The Gentlemen, Iceman with Drake, Ali Siddiq's Mondays, Steph Curry's Greatest Stories, more below that. Switch to Music and it's the same house-wide rotation playing in every room, UR Welcome Vol. 1, tracklist and all.

That's the whole of Level 27. Take the floor toggle up one and the register changes completely — this half of the Penthouse isn't for anyone but me.

The Study is the first room up there, and it's where the actual paperwork lives. Fourteen Pullen Laws sit on the left shelf, written down over eleven years, because a rule you don't write down is a rule you'll eventually forget. The first one's about arriving early. I'm not reading you the fourteenth. This week's Journal pages are out on the blotter, marked in pencil rather than ink, because nothing in this room counts as decided until it survives the pencil. Most of it doesn't.

Down the hall is the Bedroom — blackout glass, no television anywhere in it, and a lounge chair angled at the window instead of the bed, because that's where the actual thinking happens. One painting hangs behind the headboard on purpose, so I only see it when I turn around: a man on a road at dusk, walking away from whatever the artist couldn't be bothered to explain. I bought it before I could afford it, and it's hung on every wall I've had since.

Next door is the Closet, and it was never a room of clothes — it's a room of decisions I'd already made. Everything's arranged by occasion, not colour. Two dinner jackets sit front and centre, black-tie and white-tie, pressed and ready before I have to ask for either one. The door stays open more often than it's shut, which tells you how often I actually second-guess what's on those rails.

Last stop is the Bathroom — stone, brass, steam, and the whole city sitting on the other side of the glass. I don't have much to tell you about it, and that's sort of the point. Not every room needs a story attached to earn its place.

That's the tour — Kitchen to Living Room to Cinema on twenty-seven, Study to Bedroom to Closet to Bath on twenty-eight, and the Music Lounge and its bar one floor down from the Living Room whenever you want them. The arrows at either edge move you room to room. Floors, top right, doesn't list all nine — it jumps you straight to whichever level you ask for and drops you in that level's first room, which beats paging through everything just to get upstairs. And every dot you pass along the way opens the same kind of card the vault did — one line, three or four specs, nothing you have to read past. None of it's complicated. I just hadn't walked anyone through it yet, and a floor plan nobody explains might as well not exist.
